How this calculator works

The depreciable basis is the purchase price plus capitalized closing costs plus improvements made before the rental was placed in service, minus the land value, which cannot be depreciated. Residential rental property is depreciated straight line over 27.5 years, so the full-year depreciation is the basis divided by 27.5. In the first year the IRS mid-month convention treats the rental as placed in service halfway through its month, so the first-year depreciation is the full-year amount times the months left (counting the half month) over 12. Every later year gets the full amount until the basis is used up, which happens in year 28 or 29 depending on the starting month. The rules come from IRS Publication 527, Residential Rental Property (checked October 13, 2026).

Raise the land value to $90,000 and the basis drops to $216,000, cutting the full-year deduction to $7,854.55. That is why the land split matters: every dollar moved from land to building is a dollar you can depreciate.

What usually goes into basis, and what does not

At a high level, basis often includes the price, settlement costs such as title fees, recording fees and transfer taxes, and improvements made before renting. Costs tied to the loan (points, mortgage insurance) and prepaid items like property tax and insurance escrows usually do not; they are handled differently. Repairs that only keep the property in working order are generally expensed, while improvements that better, restore or adapt it are capitalized and depreciated on their own schedule. Publication 527 has the full lists, and a tax professional can confirm how your purchase breaks down.

FAQ

Why 27.5 years?

Under the IRS general depreciation system, residential rental property (a building where most of the rent comes from dwelling units) is depreciated straight line over 27.5 years. That is about 3.636% of the depreciable basis a year. Commercial property uses a different period. See IRS Publication 527 for the details and exceptions.

Why can't I depreciate the land?

Land does not wear out, so the IRS does not let you depreciate it. You subtract the land value from your cost to get the building basis. Many owners use the land-to-building split on the county property tax assessment as a starting point; an appraisal or your tax preparer can give a better number.

What is the mid-month convention?

For residential rental property, the IRS treats the building as placed in service in the middle of the month you first made it available to rent, no matter which day it was. So a rental placed in service in March gets 9.5 of 12 months of depreciation in the first year. The calculator applies that automatically from the month you enter.

Do short-term rentals use 27.5 years too?

Not always. If the average guest stay is short (commonly seven days or less), the property may not count as residential rental property for depreciation, and a different recovery period can apply. That is a question for a tax professional; this calculator only does the 27.5-year math.

What about improvements I make later?

A capital improvement made after the rental is in service (a new roof, an addition) generally starts its own depreciation schedule from the month it is placed in service. Enter only improvements made before the rental was placed in service here, and track later projects separately.

What happens when I sell?

Depreciation you took, or could have taken, can be taxed when you sell (often called depreciation recapture), and the final year is prorated by the month you sell. This calculator ignores the sale year. Talk to a tax professional before you sell.

Is my data saved or sent anywhere?

No. The math runs in your browser. Nothing is stored or sent, and refreshing the page resets the defaults.
