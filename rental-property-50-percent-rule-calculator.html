How this calculator works

Estimated operating expenses are monthly gross rent times the expense ratio (default 50%, clamped to 0–100%). Estimated NOI is rent minus those expenses; annual figures are monthly times 12. Monthly cash flow is NOI minus your mortgage principal and interest, and can be negative. Per-unit cash flow divides monthly cash flow by the number of units (blank or below 1 counts as 1, so nothing divides by zero). The max mortgage payment to break even equals the estimated NOI: any P&I above it shows negative cash flow under the rule. Negative inputs are treated as zero. The calculator does not model taxes on income, appreciation or principal paydown.

A worked example

The default numbers describe a fictional single-unit rental collecting $2,450 a month with a $1,500 monthly P&I payment and the classic 50% expense ratio.

So under the 50% rule this example runs about $275 a month short: the estimated NOI of $1,225 does not cover a $1,500 payment. To break even the P&I would need to be $1,225 or less, or rent would need to rise to $3,000 a month at the same payment. If your real expenses run at 40% instead of 50%, NOI rises to $1,470 and the shortfall shrinks to $30 a month.

When investors use the 50% rule

Buyers use the 50% rule as a second-pass screen after a rent-to-price check such as the 1% rule: it gives a fast, conservative guess at operating costs before they have a seller’s expense history. Owners revisit it when refinancing, when rents change, or when comparing a new deal against what they already hold. It is a rule of thumb, not an underwrite. A newer building with tenant-paid utilities can run well under 50%; an older building with high taxes, insurance and paid management can run above it. Once you have real figures, use a full cash-flow analysis instead of the rule.

FAQ

What is the 50% rule in real estate?

The 50% rule is a rough screen: assume about half of a rental’s gross rent goes to operating expenses (taxes, insurance, repairs, maintenance, vacancy, management, utilities you pay, CapEx reserves). The other half is an estimate of net operating income (NOI) that has to cover the mortgage. At $2,450 a month of rent, the rule estimates $1,225 of expenses and $1,225 of NOI.

Does the 50% include the mortgage payment?

No. The 50% covers operating expenses only. Mortgage principal and interest come out of the remaining half. That is why this calculator subtracts your monthly P&I from the estimated NOI to get cash flow, and shows the max payment that would break even.

Is 50% the right expense ratio for my property?

Not always. Newer buildings, tenant-paid utilities and low-tax areas can run well under 50%; older buildings, high property taxes, high insurance, owner-paid utilities and paid management can run higher. Set the expense ratio to whatever your own records or a local benchmark support. The field accepts 0–100%.

How is the 50% rule different from the 1% rule?

The 1% rule compares monthly rent to purchase price and ignores expenses entirely. The 50% rule estimates expenses and NOI from rent, then compares NOI to the mortgage. Investors often use the 1% rule first to filter listings, then the 50% rule to sanity-check cash flow, then a full cash-flow analysis with real numbers.

What does negative cash flow mean here?

It means the mortgage payment is larger than the NOI the 50% rule estimates. With the defaults, $1,500 of P&I against $1,225 of estimated NOI shows −$275 a month. It is a screening signal to look closer, not a verdict: your actual expenses may be lower or higher than the rule assumes.

Is my data saved or sent anywhere?

No. The math runs in your browser. Nothing is stored or sent, and refreshing the page resets the defaults.
