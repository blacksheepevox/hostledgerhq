How this calculator works

In percent mode the new rent is the current rent times (1 + the increase ÷ 100). In new rent mode it uses the new rent you type and works out the percent. Either way, the monthly increase is the new rent minus the current rent, the annual increase is twelve times that, and the percent increase is the monthly increase divided by the current rent. The target rent reverse answers “what percent gets me to this number?”: (target − current) ÷ current. If you enter a notice period and the date the new rent starts, it counts that many calendar days back to show the last day to send notice. A zero current rent leaves the percent fields blank; a negative percent or a lower new rent shows a decrease.

When landlords raise rent

Most increases happen at a lease renewal or, for month-to-month tenants, after written notice. Landlords usually look at rents for similar nearby units, their own cost changes such as property taxes and insurance, and what a turnover would cost: a vacant month, cleaning, repairs and marketing. Some prefer small regular increases; others hold rent steady for a long-term tenant who pays on time. Whatever you decide, put the new rent, the start date and the date you sent notice in writing.

Local rules come first. Rent control, rent stabilization and notice laws vary by state and city. Some cap how much rent can rise in a year, some require 60 or 90 days’ notice for larger increases, and some limit increases during a fixed-term lease. This page is not legal advice; confirm the rules for your property before you send a notice.

FAQ

How do I calculate a rent increase percentage?

Subtract the current rent from the new rent, divide by the current rent, and multiply by 100. Raising $1,500 to $1,600 is a $100 increase, and $100 ÷ $1,500 = 6.67%. Going the other way, a 5% increase on $1,500 is $1,500 × 1.05 = $1,575, or $75 more a month and $900 more over twelve months.

How much can a landlord raise the rent?

It depends on where the property is and what the lease says. Many places set no cap on market-rate units, but some states and a number of cities have rent control or rent stabilization rules that limit annual increases for covered units, and some require a reason or a filing. This calculator applies no caps; it only does the arithmetic. Check your state and local rules, or an attorney, before you set the number.

How much notice do I need to give before a rent increase?

Notice rules vary by state, city and lease type. A common pattern is at least 30 days’ written notice for a month-to-month tenancy, with some places requiring 60 or 90 days for larger increases or longer tenancies. For a fixed-term lease, the increase usually takes effect at renewal. The optional notice field here only counts days back from the date you enter; it does not know your local requirement.

Can I raise the rent in the middle of a lease?

Usually not on a fixed-term lease unless the lease itself allows it. Most landlords raise rent when a fixed term ends and a renewal is signed, or on a month-to-month tenancy after giving the required notice. Read the rent clause of the lease you actually signed.

What is a reasonable rent increase?

There is no single right number. Landlords often weigh market rents for similar units, their own cost changes (property taxes, insurance, utilities they pay), and the cost of turnover if the tenant leaves. A vacant month on a $1,500 unit costs $1,500, which is more than a full year of a $75-a-month increase, so the cheapest outcome is not always the highest rent.

Is my data saved or sent anywhere?

No. The math runs in your browser. Nothing is stored or sent, and refreshing the page resets the defaults.
